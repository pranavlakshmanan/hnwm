The problem with neural physics simulators isn't speed. It's trust. They can be seventy times faster than a classical solver and silently go wrong at deployment with no way to know.

We trained a single neural network to leap an arbitrary number of simulator steps ahead in one forward pass, across three very different physical systems. Then we noticed something. If you ask the network for the same future two ways, the disagreement between the two answers is a label-free signal for when its prediction can be trusted.

This page walks through the result on each system, the speed numbers, the safety net that follows from the trust signal, and a head-to-head against a 1910 textbook method that the modern version beats by a wide margin.

The trust signal, in one move

We train a single network \(f_\theta(s, T)\) that, given the current state \(s\) of a physical system, predicts the state \(T\) simulator steps later, for any \(T\) on the base–2 ladder \(\{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64\}\). The training objective is supervised:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{sup}}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{(s_0, s_T, T)\sim \mathcal{D}} \left\lVert \tilde{f}_\theta(s_0, T) - \tilde{s}_T \right\rVert_2^{2} \]

where \(\tilde{\cdot}\) denotes per-channel normalisation by training-set statistics. For any future you care about, the trained network can answer the same question two ways:

  • The direct answer: \(f_\theta(s, T)\) – jump \(T\) steps in one forward pass.
  • The doubled answer: \(f_\theta(f_\theta(s, T/2), T/2)\) – take two half–steps and compose them.

If the network has learned the dynamics correctly, the two answers must agree. Their disagreement is our trust signal:

\[ \hat{e}(s, T) \;=\; \left\lVert\, f_\theta(s, T) \;-\; f_\theta\!\left(f_\theta(s, T/2),\, T/2\right) \right\rVert_2 \]

On grid-structured states this is naturally per–cell, so \(\hat{e}\) is a spatial map highlighting exactly where the surrogate's prediction is fragile. No labels. No retraining. Two extra forward passes through the same network we already trained.

Why we have to supervise the training

A tempting shortcut would be to train the network on the self–consistency loss alone, with no ground truth:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{SC}}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{(s_0, T)\sim p} \left\lVert\, \tilde{f}_\theta(s_0, T) - \tilde{f}_\theta\!\left(f_\theta(s_0, T/2),\, T/2\right) \right\rVert_2^{2}. \]

It looks attractive because it needs no labels. But it fails, and the failure is provable. The identity map \(f_\theta(s, T) = s\) trivially satisfies the self–consistency property exactly, so it is the global minimum of \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{SC}}\) in physical state space. Gradient descent finds it in one epoch. We verified this empirically:

The lesson: ground–truth supervision is what tethers the surrogate to reality. Self–consistency is then free to act as the deployment–time probe \(\hat{e}\). The asymmetry is the whole game.

Three different physical systems, one recipe

The trust signal isn't tuned to a particular equation. We test it on three systems that span the modelling spectrum: a stiff reaction–diffusion PDE, a nonlinear hyperbolic conservation law, and a rigid–body ODE with discrete collision events. The dynamics, the natural state space, and the reference numerical method are all different. The surrogate and the probe are the same.

Environment 1 · Reaction–diffusion PDE

Belousov–Zhabotinsky waves (Oregonator)

The Oregonator model captures the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction, a real chemistry experiment where colored waves and spirals spontaneously appear and travel across a dish. Mathematically it's a two–variable reaction–diffusion PDE for activator \(u\) and inhibitor \(v\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \partial_t u &= D\,\nabla^{2} u + \tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\!\left(u(1-u) - f\,v\,\tfrac{u-q}{u+q}\right), \\ \partial_t v &= D\,\nabla^{2} v + (u - v), \end{aligned} \]

on a 256×256 periodic grid in non–dimensional units (\(D{=}1,\ \varepsilon{=}0.05,\ q{=}0.002,\ f{=}2.0\)). Reference solver: IMEX Strang–splitting, explicit finite–volume for the diffusion, implicit Euler for the reaction stiffness. Roughly 200 substeps per horizon–64 prediction.

Here the network is asked to skip 64 simulator steps ahead in one forward pass. Below: as the prediction horizon grows from \(h{=}2\) to \(h{=}64\), the trust signal \(\hat{e}\) (third panel) tracks the actual error pattern (fourth panel), without ever seeing the ground truth at deployment time.

Environment 2 · Compressible flow PDE

Euler equations in 2D

The compressible Euler equations are the math behind aerodynamics: shocks, contact discontinuities, vortex sheets. In conservative form,

\[ \partial_t \mathbf{q} + \partial_x \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{q}) + \partial_y \mathbf{G}(\mathbf{q}) \;=\; \mathbf{0}, \qquad \mathbf{q} = (\rho,\,\rho u,\,\rho v,\,E)^{\!\top}, \]

with fluxes \(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}\) determined by the equation of state \(p = (\gamma{-}1)\!\left(E - \tfrac{1}{2}\rho(u^2{+}v^2)\right)\), \(\gamma=1.4\). We solve on a 128×128 grid with shock–tube and Riemann initial conditions. Reference solver: finite–volume with HLL Riemann solver, MUSCL–Hancock reconstruction for second order in space and time, CFL–limited explicit stepping.

Environment 3 · Rigid–body ODE

3D bouncing balls

Not just PDEs. The same approach works on rigid–body physics too: balls flying around a 3D box under gravity, with near–elastic wall collisions. Between bounces, each ball obeys classical kinematics,

\[ \ddot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{g}, \qquad \dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} = \mathbf{0}, \]

and when it hits a wall, the velocity component normal to the wall flips with restitution \(e\), while the tangential component is preserved (frictionless): \(\mathbf{v}_n \to -e\,\mathbf{v}_n,\ \mathbf{v}_t \to \mathbf{v}_t\). State is the 9–vector \((\mathbf{x},\dot{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\omega})\in\mathbb{R}^{9}\). Reference solver: the closed–form analytic flow with wall–hit times resolved by quadratic root–finding, implemented in pure numpy. No contact solver, no MuJoCo.

One network, every horizon at once

The same set of weights is trained to predict horizons of 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, and 64 simulator steps simultaneously. At deployment you ask for whichever one you want.

Now the speed

At horizon 64 the surrogate runs roughly seventy times faster than the reference classical solver on the reaction–diffusion PDE, and twenty–five times faster on Euler 2D, measured on the same CPU. One neural forward pass replaces a chain of 64 sequential solver steps. Each of those steps is heavy floating–point work over a 256×256 grid. The network skips straight to the end.

Mode 2: a built–in safety net

The two deployment modes the trust signal enables.

  • Mode 1 The surrogate runs alone. Maximum speedup, no fallback. Good when average accuracy is fine and a few outliers don't matter.
  • Mode 2 Let the trust signal route the easy trajectories through the network and send the high–ê ones to the classical solver instead. You pick the threshold, you pick the cost vs accuracy point.

Trust threshold sensitivity

Mode 2 has one parameter: the gate threshold \(q\). Trajectories whose mean step–doubling score \(\bar{e}\) lands in the top \(1-q\) fraction get deferred to the classical solver; the remaining \(q\) fraction is left to the surrogate. Lower \(q\) means more deferral, which means more accuracy at the cost of more solver calls. The trade–off is smooth:

Sliding the trust threshold moves you anywhere along the speed–vs–accuracy frontier. No retraining, no extra network. One knob.

How does step doubling compare to conformal prediction?

Conformal prediction is the obvious classical baseline: take a calibration set, find the test point's nearest neighbours in some embedding, and use the empirical quantile of their errors as the predicted bound. We ran locally–adaptive split conformal with \(K{=}20\) KNN reweighting, using per–channel state statistics as the embedding.

Step doubling has a history. Here's what changes for neural surrogates.

The intuition behind \(\hat{e}\) is old. Lewis Fry Richardson worked out the classical version in 1910 for ODE solvers: run the same integration at step size \(h\) and at step size \(h/2\), compare the two answers, and the difference gives you an error estimate. Modern adaptive ODE codes still use this idea today. Our probe is the spiritual descendant. We're not claiming to have invented step doubling.

But two things change when the integrator is a neural network instead of a classical method, and both are quietly load–bearing for why this works.

1. The neural surrogate is not order-stable. Classical Richardson assumes the solver is a method of known order \(p\), so that the local error scales as \(h^{p+1}\). The \(h\)-vs-\(h/2\) difference then has a clean leading term you can read off. A neural network has no such guarantee. There is no \(p\). The naive Richardson formula doesn't apply, and empirically it collapses on these systems:

2. Multi–horizon training is what makes the probe informative. We don't just train the surrogate on the end horizon \(T\). We train it on the whole base–2 ladder \(\{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64\}\) simultaneously. That training curriculum is what teaches the network to behave consistently across scales, which is what \(\hat{e}\) is measuring. A single–horizon network would have nothing to disagree with itself about. Step doubling without multi–horizon training isn't a signal at all.

So: same intuition, completely different mechanics, completely different regime. The classical version applies when you have a method with known order. Ours applies when you've trained a neural surrogate to be consistent across scales. The proof that the surrogate generalises across three very different systems is the same network beating the textbook check at every horizon, on the same hardware, by orders of magnitude in compute.
