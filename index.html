Hybrid Neural World Models
for Physical Dynamics

Pranav Lakshmanan · Paras Chopra

One network. Any horizon. Label-free trust signal.

☰ Paper 〈/〉 Code ♦ Data

The problem with neural physics simulators isn't speed. It's trust. They can be seventy times faster than a classical solver and silently go wrong at deployment with no way to know.

We trained a single neural network to leap an arbitrary number of simulator steps ahead in one forward pass, across three very different physical systems. Then we noticed something. If you ask the network for the same future two ways, the disagreement between the two answers is a label-free signal for when its prediction can be trusted.

This page walks through the result on each system, the speed numbers, the safety net that follows from the trust signal, and a head-to-head against a 1910 textbook method that the modern version beats by a wide margin.

The trust signal, in one move

Train one network f to predict the state of a physical system at any horizon h in one shot. For any future you care about, you can now ask the same question two ways:

  • The direct answer: f(s, h) – jump h steps in one forward pass.
  • The doubled answer: f(f(s, h/2), h/2) – take two half-steps and compose.

The two answers should match. When they don't, the network is telling you it doesn't know what happens next. We call this disagreement ê, the step-doubling probe. It needs no ground-truth labels at deployment time and falls out of the same forward passes you already have to run.

Three different physical systems, one recipe

The trust signal isn't tuned to a particular equation. Below it works on a reaction–diffusion PDE, on compressible gas flow, and on rigid–body contact dynamics, all with the same training recipe and the same probe.

Environment 1 · Reaction–diffusion PDE

Belousov–Zhabotinsky waves

The Oregonator model captures the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction: a real chemistry experiment where colored waves and spirals spontaneously appear and travel across a dish. Mathematically it's a two-variable reaction–diffusion PDE on a 256×256 periodic grid.

The network is asked to skip 64 simulator steps ahead in a single forward pass. Column 4 below is what the step-doubling probe predicts the error will look like. Column 5 is the actual error against ground truth, which the network never sees at deployment. They match.

Environment 2 · Compressible flow PDE

Euler equations in 2D

The compressible Euler equations are the math behind aerodynamics: shocks, contact discontinuities, vortex sheets. Same network architecture, same training recipe, completely different physics.

Environment 3 · Rigid–body ODE

3D bouncing balls

Not just PDEs. The same approach works on rigid–body physics too: six balls flying around a 3D box under gravity with near–elastic wall collisions. Pure numpy simulator, no contact solver, no surprises.

One network, every horizon at once

The same set of weights is trained to predict horizons of 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, and 64 simulator steps simultaneously. At deployment you ask for whichever one you want.

Now the speed

At horizon 64 the surrogate runs roughly seventy times faster than the reference classical solver on the reaction–diffusion PDE, and twenty–five times faster on Euler 2D, measured on the same CPU. One neural forward pass replaces a chain of 64 sequential solver steps. Each of those steps is heavy floating–point work over a 256×256 grid. The network skips straight to the end.

Mode 2: a built–in safety net

The two deployment modes the trust signal enables.

  • Mode 1 The surrogate runs alone. Maximum speedup, no fallback. Good when average accuracy is fine and a few outliers don't matter.
  • Mode 2 Let the trust signal route the easy trajectories through the network and send the high–ê ones to the classical solver instead. You pick the threshold, you pick the cost vs accuracy point.

The deployment dial

Sliding the trust threshold moves you anywhere along the speed–vs–accuracy frontier. No retraining, no extra network. One knob.

Beating the 1910 textbook method

Step doubling has a long pedigree. Lewis Fry Richardson wrote down the classical version of this idea in 1910 for ODE solvers. We ran his check head–to–head against ours on Euler 2D, and the modern version wins on both axes.

Multi–horizon training is what makes the trust signal work where the textbook version doesn't.
